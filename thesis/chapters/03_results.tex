\section{The Individual-Level Effect Ladder}

The individual-level question is not whether the LLM is the best predictor. It
is how much of the distance from a no-information baseline to a strong tabular
model is closed by structured alignment, and where that gain sits. The main
in-distribution evaluation uses the respondent protocol restricted to the 2016,
2018, and 2019 waves: 2,726 rating rows from the 20\% hash holdout, whose
respondent numbers do not appear in \texttt{train\_r}. Baselines are HistGB, a
gradient-boosting model \citep{friedman2001gbm}, and Ridge trained on
\texttt{train\_r} with the same features, plus the base model with the same
prompts but no fine-tuning. Confidence intervals are bootstrap 95\% intervals
based on the nonparametric bootstrap \citep{efron1993bootstrap}, over rows for
MAE and correlation and over respondent--year groups for choice.

\input{tables/table2_pas}

Table~\ref{tab:pas} arranges the models as a ladder. The no-information
baseline, which predicts the training mean for every row, reaches an MAE of
32.65. Predicting each candidate's training mean instead reaches 32.35 with a
correlation of 0.155 and a choice accuracy of 0.304, so candidate identity alone
supplies some signal. The base model reaches 24.15, so prompting alone already
carries substantial signal. Fine-tuning lowers MAE to 21.35, and HistGB reaches 20.73.
The fine-tuned model therefore closes 82\% of the MAE gap between the base model
and HistGB, 66\% of the correlation gap, and 72\% of the choice gap, but it does
not close the remaining 18\% of that gap: the MAE difference is 0.62, the correlation
is 0.037 lower, and tie-aware choice also favors HistGB (0.481 versus 0.555).
Measured against the no-information baseline rather than the base model,
fine-tuning closes 95\% of the total distance to HistGB, and prompting alone
already closes 71\%; the two denominators answer different questions and both
are reported.
A second checkpoint, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, trained under the same recipe
reaches 21.37, 0.665, and 0.572 (Appendix~\ref{app:second}); the signal is not
specific to Qwen3-8B.

The paired bootstrap tells the same story. The LLM--GBM MAE difference is
$+0.59$ $[-0.17,+1.32]$ for seed 42, $+0.42$ $[-0.34,+1.19]$ for seed 43, and
$+0.89$ $[+0.10,+1.67]$ for seed 44; only the seed-44 interval excludes zero,
with the baseline never worse. The correlation difference is consistently
negative ($-0.040$, $-0.033$, and $-0.039$), and tie-aware choice consistently
favors the baseline ($-0.057$, $-0.082$, and $-0.083$). A respondent-level
cluster bootstrap on seed 42 widens the MAE interval to $[-0.40,+1.61]$ and
keeps the correlation interval negative ($[-0.062,-0.019]$). The honest reading
is that structured alignment buys a usable predictor, not a uniformly better
one.

\section{Where the Gain Sits: The Error Profile}

\input{tables/table3b_profile}

The average hides a structural pattern. Table~\ref{tab:profile} divides each
wave into true-score quartiles. In every year the LLM is better than HistGB in
Q1 and Q4, the lowest and highest true scores, and worse in Q2 and Q3, the middle
of the scale. The pattern is not a small fluctuation: in 2019 the LLM's Q1 MAE
is 13.6 against 19.5 for HistGB, and its Q4 MAE is 17.8 against 24.2, while its
Q2 and Q3 MAEs are 26.5 and 22.0 against 16.9 and 15.5. The same crossing
appears in 2016 and 2018 and in all three seeds.

The error-extreme correlation summarizes the shape. Across the in-distribution
rows, the correlation between absolute error and distance from the mean true
score is $-0.18$ for the LLM and $+0.25$ for HistGB. The two models make errors
in opposite regions: the LLM is relatively accurate at the tails and loses in
the middle; HistGB is accurate in the middle and regresses toward the mean at the
tails. This is consistent with the prediction spread: the LLM's standard
deviation is about 40, versus 24 for HistGB. The LLM does not uniformly improve
the mapping; it redistributes error from the tails to the middle, and the
average MAE is nearly unchanged because the two effects offset.

The profile is computed on the pre-repair table and should be read as a
structural observation, not as a claim that survives every preprocessing choice.
It is, however, stable across the three in-distribution waves and three seeds,
and it explains why the average comparison is a poor summary of what alignment
buys.

\section{What the LLM Adds}

Fine-tuning is necessary for the model to behave like a survey respondent rather
than a generic assistant. On the same in-distribution rows, the base LLM reaches
an MAE of 24.15, a correlation of 0.594, and a choice accuracy of 0.478; on the
2020 wave it degrades to an MAE of 44.89 and a correlation of $-0.389$.
Fine-tuning improves MAE by 2.5--3.0 relative to the base model, with a
correlation gain of about $+0.07$ and a choice gain of $+0.01$ to $+0.09$
depending on seed. This is a real change in behaviour, not a prompt artifact.

What the LLM adds is not superior point prediction. It is a single model that
consumes the same survey vignette, produces a rating distribution rather than a
point estimate, and extends to population-level generation. The price is that
the gain is localized: the LLM is better at the tails and worse in the middle,
and a feature-engineered baseline remains stronger on average and in ranking.

\section{Population-Level Results: What Was Not Bought}

For each GSS configuration (EDC and EHC), the 2024 joint distribution is
reconstructed from eight historical waves, and 400 synthetic respondents are
sampled and compared with the empirical 2024 sample (1,601 for EDC, 1,552 for
EHC). JS divergence is reported on the three marginals and the three pairwise
joints; lower is better. As a reference, we also draw 400 rows directly from the
reconstructed joint $Q^{\ast}$ without a language model.

\input{tables/table3_fas}

Direct sampling from $Q^{\ast}$ reaches a marginal JS of 0.0004 [0.0002, 0.0023] and
a joint JS of 0.0076 [0.0070, 0.0141] on EDC; on EHC it reaches 0.0011 [0.0006,
0.0036] and 0.0225 [0.0192, 0.0308]. These are lower than every LLM condition on
both metrics. The prior is therefore the strongest distributional baseline in
this task. The result is stable across sampling seeds: over repeated 400-row
draws, EDC marginal/joint JS ranges 0.0004--0.0012 / 0.0064--0.0076 and EHC
0.0011--0.0028 / 0.0190--0.0225.

The LLM results should be read against that baseline. On EDC, the base model
produces 0.0552/0.1181; direct SFT reduces these to 0.0048/0.0160 (91\%/86\%), and
v3 reaches 0.0040/0.0215 (93\%/82\%). On EHC, the base model starts at
0.0319/0.1001; direct SFT reaches 0.0167/0.0470 (48\%/53\%), and v3 reaches
0.0077/0.0363 (76\%/64\%). Fine-tuning therefore moves the LLM substantially
toward $Q^{\ast}$, but it does not surpass $Q^{\ast}$: the prior remains better on both
marginal and joint JS in both configurations. EDC is the stronger configuration
for the LLM; EHC is weaker, and the large-reduction claim is scoped to the
LLM-versus-base comparison.

Bootstrap 95\% CIs over the generated or prior-sampled rows separate the base
model from v3 on both configurations (EDC marginal 0.0552 [0.0476, 0.0644] vs 0.0040 [0.0022,
0.0077]; EHC joint 0.1001 [0.0874, 0.1188] vs 0.0363 [0.0332, 0.0458]) and
separate the prior from v3 on the joint metric (EDC 0.0076 [0.0070, 0.0141] vs
0.0215 [0.0181, 0.0305]; EHC 0.0225 [0.0192, 0.0308] vs 0.0363 [0.0332,
0.0458]). The SFT and v3 intervals overlap, so the two SFT runs are not ranked.
The full table is in Appendix~\ref{app:fas}.

Component analysis shows that RAG alone is close to the base model (EDC 0.0473,
EHC 0.0404) and that RAG plus SFT performs like direct SFT. The movement
toward $Q^{\ast}$ comes from supervised exposure to the reconstructed distribution
and the five task types, not from retrieval. Quota annealing (EDC 0.0091, EHC
0.0175) is worse than direct SFT; the quota run did not converge within 15
rounds, so the comparison is descriptive.

The base model returns 351 of 400 rows on EHC (coverage 0.877), while the SFT runs
return 399--400. Charging every missing row as maximally wrong raises the base
model's EHC marginal/joint JS from 0.0319/0.1001 to 0.1168/0.1850, while the
fully covered v3 run stays at 0.0077/0.0363; on EDC, direct SFT's 0.0048/0.0160
becomes 0.0377/0.0489, whereas v3 is unaffected at 0.0040/0.0215. The v3 run is
therefore the cleanest LLM result. On EHC, direct SFT's joint symmetric KL
increases from 0.4289 to 0.5487 even though JS falls, so we do not use it as the
EHC headline; v3 improves both JS and KL (0.0363 and 0.3064 vs 0.1001 and
0.4289), which is why v3 is the EHC reference run.
