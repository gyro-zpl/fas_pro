## 1 Introduction

Questionnaire surveys are the backbone of empirical social science and policy evaluation, but they are expensive, slow, and increasingly difficult to field at scale. Large language models (LLMs) have been proposed as virtual survey respondents: given a demographic profile and a question, a model can produce an answer without recruiting a single participant (Argyle et al., 2023; Aher et al., 2023; Dillion et al., 2023; Kim and Lee, 2023; Tourangeau et al., 2000). The promise is attractive, but most existing work evaluates the model as a one-shot prompt. A profile is placed in the context window, a question is asked, and the answer is scored. This setup measures how well a model can role-play a respondent in a single turn; it does not test whether the model has internalized the structure of survey responses, whether its outputs are coherent at the population level, or whether the behavior survives a change in the political environment.

We take a different view. Survey responses are generated by a structured process: a voter's partisan identity, ideological position, and demographics interact with a candidate's attributes, and the resulting evaluations follow regularities that are stable within an era and can shift across eras. If an LLM is to act as a credible respondent, it should be trained on that structure rather than prompted with it. We therefore build a data-centric pipeline that reconstructs voter--candidate and voter--group interactions from the American National Election Studies (ANES), aligns a compact 8B model with the resulting distribution via LoRA, and evaluates the model at both the individual and the population level. We then use the same pipeline to ask how much this alignment buys and where the gain is located.

Our first contribution is a reproducible data construction and audit. The raw ANES feature table contains three data issues that are easy to miss and that materially change results: 8,165 rows carry trailing-period candidate names (`obama.` and `obama`), of which 8,141 become duplicates after normalization and are removed; social-group thermometers (`black`, `white`, `dem`, `rep`) are stored in the same column as candidate thermometers (23,060 rows); and missing or out-of-range scores account for 3,588 rows after the group filter (the raw audit flags 1,550 missing and 2,106 out-of-range, with 68 overlapping group targets). We separate the targets and reduce 132,948 raw rows to 98,159 clean interaction-level rows. The 2016--2024 ANES waves are designed as a panel, but the released analysis table records only a within-wave respondent number; the raw `caseid` is also within-wave (cross-wave birth-year agreement 1.2--2.6%), so person-level isolation cannot be verified from these files. A temporal split therefore measures transfer to a later wave, not generalization to new individuals. We report both a temporal protocol and a respondent-held-out-number protocol, and we make the distinction explicit throughout.

Our second contribution is an effect ladder for individual-level prediction. On held-out respondent numbers in 2016--2019, a no-information baseline reaches an MAE of 32.65 and the candidate-mean baseline 32.35 with a correlation of 0.155, so candidate identity alone carries some signal; the base LLM reaches 24.15, Ridge 22.86, the fine-tuned LLM 21.35, and HistGB 20.73. Fine-tuning therefore closes 82% of the MAE gap between the base model and HistGB, 66% of the correlation gap, and 72% of the choice gap, but it does not close the remaining 18% of that gap. The gain is also not uniform: the LLM is better than HistGB at the low and high ends of the true-score distribution and worse in the middle, and the two models' error--extremity correlations have opposite signs ($-$0.184 versus $+$0.251). The average MAE hides this redistribution of error; the error profile is the more informative summary.

Our third contribution is a population-level evaluation. On the GSS policy-attitude data, fine-tuning moves the model toward a reconstructed joint distribution, but direct sampling from that prior is more accurate: on EDC the prior reaches marginal and joint Jensen--Shannon divergences of 0.0004 and 0.0076, versus 0.0040 and 0.0215 for the best fine-tuned run. The LLM's movement toward the prior is driven by supervised fine-tuning, not by retrieval or quota sampling. The prior remains the stronger distributional baseline; the LLM does not beat it on unconditional distribution matching.

Our fourth contribution is a validity analysis of the temporal comparison. The per-year table appears to decay from 0.722 in 2019 to $-$0.056 in 2024, but it is not a clean four-year test: 2020 has no 5-point ideology item and a reversed partisanship scale, 2022 is a validation wave with only 194 held-out rows and protocol-dependent results, and 2024 loses its political covariates in the processed table. A covariate-dropout probe on 2019 reproduces most of the 2024 loss, and the raw 2024 file contains the items as text labels. We therefore treat temporal generalization as unproven, not as a demonstrated failure, and argue that a covariate audit must precede any temporal claim.
