## 10 Conclusion

We presented a data-centric pipeline for LLM survey simulation and evaluated it at three levels: the individual respondent, the population, and time. The pipeline reconstructs interaction-level data from ANES, decouples candidate identity from candidate attributes, encodes partisan and ideological structure as explicit features, samples the training pool to cover tails and conflict cases, and fine-tunes a compact 8B model with LoRA. The same pipeline reconstructs a joint distribution from GSS and fine-tunes a model to generate a synthetic population.

The individual-level result is deliberately modest. On held-out respondent numbers in the same era, the fine-tuned model reaches an MAE of 21.35, a correlation of 0.666, and a choice accuracy of 0.525 against a chance level of 0.163. A gradient-boosted baseline on the same features reaches 20.73, 0.703, and 0.543. The MAE difference is small and seed-dependent, and the baseline has a higher correlation and a tie-aware choice advantage. The value of the language model is not superior point prediction; it is a unified interface that produces a rating distribution and extends to population-level generation.

The population-level result is a boundary rather than a win. Fine-tuning moves the LLM toward the reconstructed joint distribution (EDC marginal/joint JS 0.0048/0.0160 for direct SFT; 0.0040/0.0215 for v3), but direct sampling from that prior is more accurate (0.0004/0.0076). The LLM approaches the prior without surpassing it; its value must lie in conditional generation or natural-language control, which the current evaluation does not isolate.

The temporal result is the most important negative finding. Individual-level correlation falls from 0.722 in 2019 to $-$0.056 in 2024, and the tabular baseline collapses in the same way. A constant prediction of 50 has an MAE of 35.05, so the aggregate 2024 MAE is not evidence of individual signal. Adding the most recent wave to training does not help. The failure is in the survey distribution, not in the language model alone. Structured alignment is necessary but not sufficient for temporal generalization, and temporal shift should be treated as a first-class evaluation axis.

Future work has four directions. First, replicate the pipeline on a broader set of model families to test whether the individual-level tie with the tabular baseline is model-specific. Second, extend the evaluation to cross-national surveys and to the group-attitude corpus. Third, develop temporal adaptation methods---recency-weighted training, domain adaptation, or uncertainty-aware decoding---and evaluate them on the 2024 wave that defeats the current model. Fourth, study the choice metric against observed vote choice, so that rating-induced choice can be validated against behavior. We hope that the negative results reported here make the next round of work easier to calibrate.
