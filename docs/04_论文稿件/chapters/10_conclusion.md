## 10 Conclusion

We presented a data-centric benchmark for LLM survey simulation and asked what structured alignment buys, and where. The pipeline reconstructs interaction-level ANES data, decouples candidate identity from attributes, encodes partisan and ideological structure as explicit features, samples the training pool to cover tails and conflict cases, and fine-tunes a compact 8B model with LoRA. The same pipeline reconstructs a GSS joint distribution and fine-tunes a model to generate a synthetic population.

The answer has three layers. **How much**: fine-tuning lowers MAE from 24.15 for the base model to 21.35 and closes 79.5% of the MAE gap to HistGB at 20.63, 66.8% of the correlation gap, and 78.0% of the choice gap. It buys a usable predictor, not a uniformly better one. **Where**: the gain is U-shaped. The LLM is better than HistGB at the low and high ends of the true-score distribution and worse in the middle, and the two models' error--extremity correlations have opposite signs ($-$0.184 versus $+$0.232). The average MAE hides this redistribution of error; the error profile is the more informative summary. **What was not bought**: a tabular baseline remains stronger on average and in ranking, a classical prior remains stronger at unconditional population matching, and temporal generalization is not established. The apparent 2024 collapse is dominated by covariate loss in the processed table, not by a clean four-year shift.

The population-level result is a boundary rather than a win. Fine-tuning moves the LLM toward the reconstructed joint distribution (EDC marginal/joint JS 0.0048/0.0160 for direct SFT; 0.0040/0.0215 for v3), but direct sampling from that prior is more accurate (0.0004/0.0076). The LLM approaches the prior without surpassing it; its value must lie in conditional generation or natural-language control, which the current evaluation does not isolate.

The lasting result is a boundary map and a validity checklist rather than a claim of LLM superiority. Structured alignment is necessary but not sufficient: it changes which cases the model handles well, not the average ranking of model classes. A credible survey simulator must be evaluated against no-information, prompt-only, tabular, and prior baselines; must report where the error sits; and must audit person linkage and covariate availability before interpreting a temporal difference.

Future work has four directions. First, replicate the benchmark on more model families to test whether the error profile is model-specific. Second, extend the evaluation to cross-national surveys and the group-attitude corpus. Third, retrain on a fully repaired feature table and isolate conditional generation and natural-language control. Fourth, obtain a cross-wave person key and validate rating-induced choice against observed vote choice. We hope that the boundary results reported here make the next round of work easier to calibrate.
